# S1 · Photosynthesis handout (week 5)

## [§1] Photosynthesis: how plants capture light

[¶1] Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae and cyanobacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in sugars. Because these organisms make their own food from inorganic molecules, they are called autotrophs, or producers. Almost every food chain on Earth starts with them, and the oxygen in the air we breathe was released by photosynthesis.

[¶2] The overall reaction can be summarized as: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂, driven by light energy. Carbon dioxide and water go in; glucose and oxygen come out. This equation is only a summary of many smaller steps, which happen in two linked stages.

### [§2] Where photosynthesis happens

[¶3] In plants, photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts, mostly in the mesophyll cells of leaves. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf through small pores called stomata, and water arrives from the roots through the xylem. A chloroplast has a double membrane around a fluid called the stroma. Inside the stroma are flattened membrane sacs called thylakoids, which are stacked into piles called grana.

[¶4] The green pigment chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membranes. It absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.

### [§3] Stage 1: the light-dependent reactions

[¶5] The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes. Light energy excites electrons in chlorophyll molecules grouped into photosystems. The excited electrons are passed along an electron transport chain, and the energy they release is used to pump protons (H⁺) into the space inside the thylakoid. The protons then flow back out through the enzyme ATP synthase, which uses this flow to make ATP. At the end of the chain, the electrons are used to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH.

[¶6] The electrons that chlorophyll loses must be replaced. They come from splitting water molecules, a step called photolysis: 2 H₂O → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ + O₂. The oxygen released by photosynthesis is a by-product of this step, so it comes from water and not from carbon dioxide. Experiments that labelled water with a heavy isotope of oxygen showed that the labelled oxygen ended up in the O₂ that was released.

### [§4] Stage 2: the Calvin cycle

[¶7] The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. It does not use light directly, so it is sometimes called the light-independent reactions, but it depends on the ATP and NADPH made in stage 1. For that reason it stops soon after a plant is put in the dark. The cycle has three phases. In carbon fixation, the enzyme Rubisco attaches a molecule of CO₂ to a five-carbon sugar called RuBP. The unstable six-carbon product immediately splits into two three-carbon molecules of 3-PGA.

[¶8] In the reduction phase, ATP and NADPH convert 3-PGA into a three-carbon sugar called G3P: NADPH supplies the electrons and ATP supplies the energy. In the regeneration phase, most of the G3P is used, with more ATP, to rebuild RuBP so the cycle can continue. For every three molecules of CO₂ fixed, one molecule of G3P leaves the cycle. Plants use it to build glucose, sucrose, starch and other organic molecules. Making one glucose molecule needs two G3P, which means six turns of the cycle.

### [§5] Limiting factors

[¶9] The rate of photosynthesis depends mainly on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. Whichever of these is in shortest supply is the limiting factor: increasing the other two will not speed up photosynthesis until the limiting factor is increased. Temperature matters because the Calvin cycle depends on enzymes such as Rubisco. The rate rises with temperature up to an optimum and then falls as the enzymes start to denature.

[¶10] For interest only (not examined): in the 1600s, Jan van Helmont grew a willow tree in a pot of weighed soil for five years. The tree gained a great deal of mass while the soil lost almost none, and he concluded that the new mass came from water. He was partly right; we now know that most of a plant's dry mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air.

[¶11] Exam tip from the lecturer: you must be able to compare the light-dependent reactions and the Calvin cycle by location, inputs and outputs, and explain why the Calvin cycle stops in the dark.
